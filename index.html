Regulating Business in America: A Long-Term Perspective

Prof. William James (Jim) Adams · Arthur F. Thurnau Professor Emeritus, Professor Emeritus of Economics, and former Peterson Professor of Industrial Organization · University of Michigan

Since the Civil War, the United States has experienced periodic waves of regulatory and deregulatory fervor. The pro-regulation tendency is associated with the Progressive agendas of the two Roosevelts (Teddy and Franklin), while the anti-regulation tendency is associated with the Supreme Courts of the late-nineteenth and late-twentieth centuries. Drawing on the regulation of business in different types of markets (labor markets, industrial markets, public utilities) and different types of regulatory institution (legislatures, administrative agencies), this lecture will explore the economic and legal ideas, as well as the economic realities, that shaped these turns in American regulatory history.

About the speaker

Dr. William James (Jim) Adams is Arthur F. Thurnau Professor Emeritus, Professor Emeritus of Economics, and the former Peterson Professor of Industrial Organization at the University of Michigan. His research has ranged broadly, from public policies toward business in the United States to economic integration in Europe. His monograph, Restructuring the French Economy: Government and the Rise of Market Competition since World War II, has been called “one of the most penetrating [and] comprehensive analyses of French contemporary economic reality.” Adams has advised the Federal Trade Commission, the U.S. Ambassador to France, and several foreign policy departments of the U.S. government. He is a winner of a Golden Apple, awarded each year by students to the best teacher at the University of Michigan.

The Pursuit of Happiness

Elizabeth Anderson, Ph.D. · MacArthur Fellow, Fellow of the British Academy, and John Dewey Distinguished University Professor of Philosophy and Women’s Studies · University of Michigan

John Locke wrote that people possess the rights of life, liberty, and property. Jefferson took that idea and changed a single word. Anderson isn’t putting words in his mouth; she’s carrying his argument to its logical conclusion.

Thursday Lecture Series October 1, 2026 · 10:00 - 11:30 am

Why Policymaking Success Leads to Electoral Failure

Matt Grossmann · Director of the Institute for Public Policy and Social Research and Professor of Political Science · Michigan State University

Are policymakers rewarded in elections when they succeed in moving public policy in their ideological direction? Or do they face a thermostatic backlash, as citizens judge their policy moves as too hot or too cold? My analysis of Congressional election outcomes since 1948 adds information on Congressional policy actions to traditional election models emphasizing the surge and decline of presidential support and referendums based on presidential approval and the economy. I find that the electorate reacts against the ideological direction of policy, voting against parties that pushes policy further to the left or to the right in both midterm and presidential years. Even after accounting for policy and traditional explanations, however, there remains a large midterm penalty for the president’s party.

About the speaker

Matt Grossmann is Director of the Institute for Public Policy and Social Research and Professor of Political Science at Michigan State University. He also serves as a Senior Fellow at the Niskanen Center and host of the Science of Politics podcast. He is an author of The Not-So-Special Interests, Artists of the Possible, Asymmetric Politics (with David Hopkins), Red State Blues, How Social Science Got Better, and Polarized by Degrees (also with David). He is co-owner of Hooked (a bookstore/cafe). His forthcoming book is Policymaking for Realists.

In 1822 the fur trader Alexis St. Martin suffered a shotgun wound on Mackinaw Island. He was attended by the physician William Beaumont. About this catalog

Compiled from the printed OLLI-UM catalog brochures (Fall 2018 through Winter 2026; not every season's brochure has surfaced yet), supplemented with OLLI's lecture-announcement emails (2017–2026), which fill in individual talks for seasons whose brochures listed only series themes — and for seasons with no surviving brochure at all. Email-sourced entries say so in their notes. Some entries note misprints found in the sources. Known gaps:

  • Fall 2024: the 'Limitations on the Exercise of Free Expression' Thursday series (Oct-Nov 2024) is announced series-level only; its individual lectures are not printed anywhere in the PDF.
  • Winter/Spring 2025: the 'Celebrating the Beauty and Power of the Arts' Thursday series (Mar 13 - Apr 3, 2025) is announced series-level only ('More details to come'); individual lectures are not printed anywhere in the PDF.
  • Fall 2018: Distinguished lectures print no descriptions — only date, title, and speaker credentials; only the Sep 2018-Jan 2019 half of the ten-lecture season is printed, and no Summer series page exists in this catalog.
  • Fall 2018: Thursday Morning series print themes only; individual lectures are not printed (same shape as the 2023 editions).
  • Fall 2019: only a 5-page lecture-pages excerpt was received (Microsoft Print-to-PDF, body text rasterized — no text layer; records transcribed visually in-session, BLC-010). Study groups, events, and all other catalog sections are not in the excerpt.
  • Fall 2019: only the Sep 2019-Jan 2020 half of the ten-lecture Distinguished season is printed in the received pages (same split as Fall 2018).
  • Fall 2019: Thursday series print themes only; individual lectures are not printed (same shape as Fall 2018).
  • Fall 2019: the theme list announces ‘Water in Michigan (Summer 2020)’ name-only; no series page exists in the received excerpt — no record.
  • Fall 2020: the theme list announces “Food Conservancy (Summer 2021)” name-only; the series is fully described (as Food Insecurity) only in the Winter/Spring 2021 catalog, where its record lives.
  • Winter/Spring 2021: Thursday series print themes only; the sponsored individual lectures (Gravitational Waves 1/21, John Kingston 2/11, Graphics and Picture Books for Children 4/1) are title/date mentions without speakers or descriptions — no lecture records.
  • Winter/Spring 2021: Torn From the Headlines prints no topics or speakers (announced shortly before each session) — series record only.
  • Fall 2022 (final catalog v3, 33pp): only the Sep 2022-Jan 2023 Distinguished lectures and two Thursday themes are printed; no DEI/Zekelman lecture pages exist in this file.
